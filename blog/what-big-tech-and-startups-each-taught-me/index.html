What Big Tech and Startups Each Taught Me

March 2026

People usually compare big tech and startups on prestige, compensation, stability and speed. Those matter, but the difference that stayed with me is what each one trained in me. Bloomberg and Amazon taught me how to build within structure. Macro, a startup, taught me how to build without it.

At Bloomberg, it was obvious early that writing code was only one part of the job. A lot of the work was reading code I didn’t write, understanding systems I didn’t design, and changing codebases already shaped by years of decisions made before I got there. There, and later at Amazon, even a task that looked small sat inside more context than I could see: existing patterns and architecture, existing assumptions, downstream dependencies, operational concerns, team norms, review expectations. A local fix wasn’t necessarily a good fix. “Does it work?” was still necessary, but it was no longer the full standard.

Part of that came from the workflow around the code: planning, review, testing, deployment, design discussions, cross-team coordination. Early on I experienced that as friction, distance between the idea and the work. Later I understood that much of it was accumulated caution: the cost of working on systems that already carried history and other people’s trust. When more people are involved, more systems are connected and more customers depend on the software, care has to show up earlier. At Amazon, I also had to explain my thinking, defend tradeoffs and anticipate review questions. That taught me patience and discipline, and to respect the idea that good engineering is often shaped by structure long before anyone writes code.

That changed what rigor meant to me. It used to sound like a polished way of saying “be careful.” In those environments it was concrete. Systems have memory. Decisions outlive the person who made them. Rigor meant asking whether something fit, what it assumed, what would break at the edges once other people depended on it, and what it would cost someone else to maintain.

It also taught me humility. When you join a system with history, your job at first is learning before changing. You’re not walking into a blank canvas. You’re walking into other people’s reasoning, tradeoffs and mistakes, some of which still make sense and some of which no longer do. I got slower to assume I understood a problem from the surface, and more likely to ask why a system looks the way it does before deciding it should change.

Some of my first instincts still come from those years. I look for edge cases. I ask whether an abstraction belongs. I worry about long-term maintainability. I care whether a change fits the rest of the system, not just whether it succeeds in isolation.

At Amazon, I worked on a two-pizza team, the idea that a team shouldn’t be bigger than what two pizzas could feed. Macro was a two-pizza company, and much less had been decided for me. The boundaries were looser, and there was less process between an idea and a decision, and between a decision and the code. In bigger companies, the shape of the work was mostly set by the time it reached me. At Macro, the shape of the work was part of the work.

The work was also more immediate. There was less ceremony and less insulation. That directness was energizing. The engineering felt broader, but also more exposed.

That meant implementation and product judgment were hard to separate. The technical question was rarely the only one. I spent much more time asking whether something was useful, whether users would care, whether this was the right version of the idea, and whether the company should be investing in this direction yet. I was helping set scope, pressure-testing whether an idea was good, and deciding what version was worth shipping now, knowing some of those calls would be revised as soon as reality showed up.

So “good” started one step earlier. Before asking whether something was well designed, you had to ask whether it was worth building. Sometimes the most valuable thing wasn’t the most polished version of an idea but the fastest honest version, so the company could learn something real.

The product direction, the scope, sometimes the technical direction too, could all be moving at once. The right answer often wasn’t waiting to be found through enough careful analysis. You made a reasonable call, moved forward and learned from what happened next.

Parts of this felt like school again: build directly, figure it out in motion. The difference was that the product was real, the users were real, and so were the consequences of a wrong assumption. Moving fast at a startup doesn’t mean moving carelessly. In some ways it demands more judgment, because there is less process protecting you from your own assumptions. When I say Macro taught me to build without structure, I don’t mean without standards or without good engineering. I mean without settled process, institutional memory and predefined boundaries around the work.

Each environment’s strengths were tied to its limitations. Big companies run on continuity. The systems have history, the workflows have been refined, the people around the work carry context, and there’s a clearer path from idea to deployment. When that structure matches the problem, it’s very effective.

But a system refined over years is strong partly because it has habits, and habits are hard to change. When the assumptions underneath the work shift, the same workflows can keep working very well for a version of the problem that is no longer the one in front of you.

Startup work has the opposite shape. There’s little continuity to rely on and little distance between building something and learning whether it matters. The work is less about fitting into a machine that already exists and more about figuring out what the machine needs to become. The cost is that you can build quickly, think carefully and still throw away a surprising amount of work because the company learned something new or a different priority suddenly mattered more.

Progress at a big company often felt more durable. At a startup it felt more provisional.

I don’t see big tech and startups as opposites anymore. When I look at a problem now, I still ask the big-company questions first: will this hold up, does it fit, what hidden costs are we creating, what happens when other people depend on this? Then I ask the startup ones: is this useful, is this the right version of the idea, what will we learn by shipping it, is it worth building now? One environment gave me system judgment, the other product judgment, and each made me see something the other could not.
